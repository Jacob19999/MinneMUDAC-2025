Evidence of Mentors who received the required two-hour, curriculum-based training before or within one month of the match start are likely better prepared, enhancing match quality.
Commitment to an 18-month relationship, indicates stability and potential for positive outcomes.
Matches based on shared interests or dislikes, with mentor preferences considered.
Monthly in-person or phone support from agency staff to mentors, youth, and parents correlates with longer, higher-quality matches
High mentor satisfaction and realistic expectations about the relationship.
Positive youth-reported relationship quality and frequent meetings.
Mentor-youth demographic (Race, gender, religion) alignment suggest strong, lasting match.
Training from BBB, mentee parental involvement with mentor, and matching based on shared interests or similarities enhance quality and longevity. Ongoing program support repairs conflicts.
Evidence Close geographic location between mentor and mentee or access to readily available transportation if mentor don’t have a car.
Positive Youth Development constructs (e.g., the five Cs: competence, confidence, connection, care, character) and thriving-oriented activities mediate better outcomes, enhancing relationship quality. Strong parent-child relationships can also amplify mentoring benefits.
Mentor is older, experienced mentors with positive attitudes toward youth, high self-efficacy, and multicultural competence foster stronger, longer matches. Empathy and flexibility are key.
Mentees with better relational histories, positive personality traits, and moderate risk status show higher relationship quality. Younger mentees (elementary to early adolescence) form closer bonds.

Red Flags to Detect:
Lack of pre-match training or inadequate ongoing support.
Mismatches in interests, especially if mentors reject youth preferences.
Infrequent or superficial staff check-ins (e.g., under 6 minutes).
Signs of mentor frustration, unrealistic expectations, or youth resistance without support.
Absence of closure plans or meetings if the match is ending prematurely.

Mentor is younger (18–25) or those with negative attitudes, low commitment, or cultural insensitivity often struggle, risking early termination.
Mentee is Older adolescents seeking autonomy or those with severe risk factors (e.g., family instability) may resist connection, increasing termination risk.
Absence of monthly staff support contacts (or reliance on email rather than phone/in-person) is linked to reduced match quality and duration
BBB provided inadequate training to mentor, or excessive/scanty staff involvement heighten closure risks. Mentee Parental dissatisfaction or interference with mentor can destabilize matches.
Relationships ending before 13–18 months may indicate instability or dissatisfaction, undermining potential benefits.

Supplement:
  Relationship Characteristics (RQ1) 
Green Flags: Long-lasting relationships (≥12 months) with consistent, frequent interactions correlate with improved mentee outcomes (e.g., self-worth, academic competence, reduced behavioral issues). High-quality relationships feature support, trust, sensitivity, and a balanced mix of emotional, recreational, and goal-oriented activities. Structured yet flexible interactions enhance perceived mentor commitment.
Red Flags: Premature termination (e.g., <6 months) is common (34–50% of matches) and linked to decreased self-worth and scholastic competence. Early closures often stem from dissatisfaction, unrealistic expectations, or poor termination management, leading to feelings of rejection or distrust in mentees.
  Measurement Approaches (RQ2) 
Relationship quality is assessed via adapted scales (e.g., from psychotherapy) or mentoring-specific tools, focusing on trust, closeness, and support. However, inconsistent validation limits precision, suggesting caution in over-relying on self-reports without multiple perspectives.
  Mediating Processes (RQ3) 
Green Flags: Positive Youth Development constructs (e.g., the five Cs: competence, confidence, connection, care, character) and thriving-oriented activities mediate better outcomes, enhancing relationship quality. Strong parent-child relationships can also amplify mentoring benefits.
Red Flags: Lack of engagement in thriving activities or poor mentor-mentee closeness may weaken developmental gains.
  Moderating Variables (RQ4) 
Mentor Characteristics: 
Green Flags: Older, experienced mentors with positive attitudes toward youth, high self-efficacy, and multicultural competence foster stronger, longer matches. Empathy and flexibility are key.
Red Flags: Younger mentors (18–25) or those with negative attitudes, low commitment, or cultural insensitivity often struggle, risking early termination.
Mentee Characteristics: 
Green Flags: Mentees with better relational histories, positive personality traits, and moderate risk status show higher relationship quality. Younger mentees (elementary to early adolescence) form closer bonds.
Red Flags: Older adolescents seeking autonomy or those with severe risk factors (e.g., family instability) may resist connection, increasing termination risk.
Program Practices & Matching: 
Green Flags: Training, parental involvement, and matching based on shared interests or similarities enhance quality and longevity. Ongoing program support repairs conflicts.
Red Flags: Poor matching (e.g., significant dissimilarities), inadequate training, or excessive/scanty staff involvement heighten closure risks. Parental dissatisfaction or interference can destabilize matches.
  Practical Implications 
Programs should prioritize mentor maturity, training for empathy and goal-setting, and careful matching. Proper closure processes mitigate harm from terminations. Longitudinal research is needed to refine these indicators.
